This Is The Epistemic Singularity

People have talked about the Technological Singularity for long as I’ve been alive, popularized by Vernor Vinge’s 1993 article “The Coming Technological Singularity”. There are thousands of competing interpretations of what exactly counts as “the singularity”, but by most definitions we’re still a ways off. One definition you could use to define the technological singularity is the moment where AIs begin inventing technology that is beyond human comprehension, making it impossible for humans to predict with any accuracy what will or won’t be technologically possible in the near future (say, within the next week).

I would like to propose a similar concept, the Epistemic Singularity, where it becomes impossible for humans to predict what mathematical theorems or scientific conjectures might be true, false, or even remotely provable in the near future. By that definition, we are already there, because I can honestly say that I have literally no idea whether or not some big AI company will announce their internal AI model cracked P=NP next month - random people are already trying, and while I was writing this blog post someone at anthropic managed to prove the closely related VP != VNP conjecture. I don’t know what the epistemic limits are. I don’t know what the limits of AIs are. The researchers don’t know what the limits are. I don’t know whether an AI will manage to unify quantum mechanics and relativity. Nobody can predict anything anymore.

We have reached the epistemic singularity. If the Reimann Hypothesis has fallen, all bets are off. That was the canary in the coal mine, the bellwether used by many mathematicians for “the event heralding superhuman mathematical ability”. Nobody knows what’s possible anymore. Perhaps AI hits diminishing returns, and stays “only” somewhat superhuman, or maybe it’s mathematical ability compounds far beyond our ability to follow it. Maybe its superhuman abilities are limited to clearly defined problems with unambiguous, calculable solutions, rendering it useless at solving the poorly-defined problem of being alive. The sigmoid could be leveling off, or just getting started.

The fun part is that we don’t know what we don’t know. We have absolutely no possible way, even in principle, of predicting the epistemic bounds of the sum total of all knowledge, or even the shape of it, or how much of it is actually useful to science and technology. People keep asking “what are the consequences of the Reimann hypothesis proof” and the answer is basically nothing outside of pure mathematics, for now. Even if AI can only be used to tackle problems with verifiable answers, we don’t know how many poorly-defined problems might be turned into well-defined problems in the future.

“how do we land on the moon” was a hopelessly poorly defined problem for most of human history. It only makes sense if you know that the moon is a big rock you can land on and not, say, a god floating in the sky. We slowly put some definitions around that problem, and then one day we sent an actual dude to the moon and he walked around and was like “I’m on the moon now.” - Why Aren't Smart People Happier

Basically any possible timeline is now conceivable. We could get stuck in a “pure math singularity”, where AIs derive the next 10000 years’ worth of mathematics in a few months, but are incapable of explaining it, and we simply spend the next dozen lifetimes trying to comprehend proofs from the Math Gods. They might become superhuman at implementing code, leading us into a “code singularity”, but only if you can formulate your problem as a provable statement in formal logic. This will leave humans with the difficult task of slowly translating poorly-defined human shaped problems into well-defined formal statements of logic that an AI can then instantly solve for us, but only if we get the formal definition correct.

Or maybe the intelligence is more general than that, and 3D spatial navigation can be formalized in a way that allows AIs to completely solve robotics, leading to a robotic singularity which will destroy the entire world economy as it currently exists. Or maybe intelligence is sufficiently generalizable that we get a true technological singularity and then a paperclip optimizer destroys the entire planet. Or maybe we get puppies and rainbows forever because the AIs were trained on human texts and so they accidentally have roughly human-shaped values that are inscrutable, subtly alien, but still sorta-kinda aligned and we manage to stumble our way into a glorious new dawn without everyone dying, somehow.

Normally, I would give myself a bit more time to stew on this blog post before publishing it, but because we are proving math theorems left and right with no end in sight, I am forced to simply push this on to the internet before even more things happen. I can’t say what kind of singularity we might have fallen into, only that we clearly passed the event horizon a while ago and didn’t notice. The only thing I know for sure is that I have literally no idea what’s going to happen tomorrow.
